You have put in the work. The problems are behind you. The techniques are yours.

What follows is not more preparation. It is a reminder of what to do with everything you have built when you are in the room --- and what to do after you leave it.

</GoldBox>

# The Opening Minutes

Most quant interviews begin with 2--3 minutes of conversation before any technical problem. This is not small talk --- it is part of the evaluation. Impressions form early and colour everything that follows.

## Tell me about yourself

Prepare a 90-second answer. The structure that works:

1.  **Background.** Degree, institution, relevant focus --- one sentence.

2.  **The pivot.** A concrete moment that directed you toward quant finance. Specific, not generic.

3.  **Why this firm.** One genuine reason. Research their work beforehand.

Vague motivation signals low preparation. Specificity signals both self-awareness and effort. Practise out loud until it sounds natural.

## Conveying genuine enthusiasm

Top firms choose between candidates who are all technically strong. Genuine curiosity about the mathematics --- not just the compensation --- is a real differentiator. It shows, and experienced interviewers notice it.

# The Resume Walkthrough

"Walk me through your resume" is frequently the entry point to the hardest technical questions in the interview.

<GrayBox>

**The rule:** know every line well enough to go three levels deep. *Stochastic processes* $\to$ be ready to prove the optional stopping theorem. *Machine learning* $\to$ bias--variance from first principles. *Quantitative research* $\to$ methodology, results, and limitations under sustained questioning. Do not list anything you cannot defend.

</GrayBox>

For any project: prepare a two-minute verbal summary covering the problem, your specific contribution, and one key quantitative result. "Improved prediction accuracy by 12%" is remembered. "Improved the model" is not.

# Thinking Out Loud

Silence is the most common and most costly mistake.

Interviewers evaluate how you think, not only whether you reach the right answer. A candidate who narrates clear reasoning and reaches a wrong answer will often advance over one who produces the correct answer in silence. **The reasoning is the signal.**

- **State your assumptions** before you begin.

- **Name the technique** before you apply it. "I think this is a linearity-of-expectation problem..." is far more valuable than a silent derivation.

- **Estimate first.** Say what you expect the answer to look like before working through the algebra. It demonstrates intuition and gives you a sanity check.

- **Narrate the stuck.** "I know the stationary distribution satisfies $\pi P = \pi$, so let me set up the system directly." Interviewers give hints --- but only if they know where you are.

Never apologise for taking time to think. A brief pause to structure your approach is discipline, not weakness.

# Questions to Ask

A candidate who asks nothing signals low engagement. Avoid questions answerable by reading the firm's website. The best questions reveal genuine curiosity:

- *"What does the research process look like from idea to live deployment?"*

- *"What distinguishes the candidates who ramp fastest in their first year?"*

- *"What are the hardest open problems your team is working on?"*

One substantive question per interviewer is the right cadence. End every interview with:

<GrayBox>

*"Is there anything from our conversation today that you would like me to clarify or expand on?"*

</GrayBox>

Almost no candidate asks this. It gives you a genuine second chance on anything that landed poorly, and it signals the intellectual honesty that distinguishes strong candidates.

# After the Interview

Within an hour: write down every problem you were asked, your approach, and where you felt uncertain. Do this before the adrenaline fades. Real problems from the actual room are the most efficient source of targeted practice you will ever have.

**On rejection.** Rejection from a top firm is not evidence that you are incapable of a quant career. It means you were not the strongest candidate in a particular pool, at a particular moment. The quant field has dozens of excellent employers. If you get an offer you respect, take time to evaluate it carefully --- the interview is the beginning of a relationship, not the end of a decision.
